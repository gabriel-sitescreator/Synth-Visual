What Is a VPN? How It Works and What It's Used For

A clear, practical introduction to virtual private networks, online privacy, encrypted connections, and what a VPN can—and cannot—protect.

The quick answer

A Virtual Private Network (VPN) routes traffic through a VPN server and typically encrypts the connection between your device and that server. This can help protect traffic on local networks and hide your original public IP address from the websites you visit—but it does not make you completely anonymous.

A Virtual Private Network (VPN) is a technology that creates an encrypted connection between your device and a VPN server, helping protect your internet traffic and conceal your original public IP address from the websites you visit.

People use VPNs for several reasons, including improving online privacy, adding protection when connecting to public Wi-Fi, accessing workplace networks remotely, and making their internet traffic appear to come from a different location.

However, a VPN is not a complete security solution. It does not automatically prevent hackers, block every tracking method, or make you anonymous online.

Understanding how this technology works can help you decide whether a VPN is useful for your everyday internet activities.

What Does VPN Stand For?

VPN stands for Virtual Private Network.

Each word describes an important part of the technology:

  • Virtual: The connection is created using software and existing network infrastructure rather than requiring a dedicated physical connection between every device and network.
  • Private: The connection can protect information traveling through a public network, such as the internet, from being easily read by unauthorized parties.
  • Network: Connected devices communicate with other devices, servers, or private systems through an established connection.

According to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), a VPN is a virtual network built on existing network infrastructure that can provide secure communication between devices or networks.

In everyday terms, think of a VPN as a protected route through the internet. Instead of sending your internet traffic directly toward its destination, your device sends it through a VPN server using an encrypted connection.

The VPN server then forwards the traffic toward the destination.

The process adds a layer of protection to the connection, but the amount of privacy you gain depends on the VPN's implementation, its settings, and the provider operating it.

How Does a VPN Work?

To understand how a VPN works, it helps to know what normally happens when you visit a website.

Without a VPN, your device connects through your internet service provider (ISP), which helps route your traffic to its destination. Depending on encryption, network configuration, and the information involved, your ISP and other intermediaries may be able to observe certain details about your connection.

A VPN changes this process by creating an encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN server.

Here's how it works step by step.

1

Your Device Connects to a VPN Server

You open a VPN application or activate a VPN connection configured on your device. The application establishes a connection to a VPN server operated by the provider or your organization. This server acts as an intermediary between your device and the destination you want to reach.

2

Your Internet Traffic Travels Through an Encrypted Tunnel

Once the VPN connection is established, the VPN software encrypts traffic carried through the tunnel. Encryption transforms readable information into a form that cannot normally be understood without the appropriate key. This helps protect the traffic from someone monitoring the local network, including other people connected to the same public Wi-Fi network. Protection depends on the VPN implementing encryption correctly and routing the relevant traffic through the tunnel. Some configurations allow selected traffic to bypass the VPN.

3

The VPN Server Forwards Your Traffic

Your traffic travels through the internet to the VPN server. The server processes the VPN connection and forwards your request to the intended destination, such as a website or an online service. If the destination uses HTTPS, the browser-to-website connection also has its own encryption. HTTPS protection can continue through the VPN server to the website, so the provider does not automatically gain access to the contents of properly encrypted HTTPS pages. If a connection uses an unencrypted protocol, information may be exposed after it leaves the VPN tunnel.

4

The Website Sees the VPN Server's Public IP Address

An IP address is a numerical address used to identify a device or network connection on the internet. When you use a VPN, websites typically see the public IP address of the VPN server forwarding your request, rather than your original public IP address.

For example, imagine you are at home in New York and connect to a VPN server in London. When you visit a website, it may see a public IP address associated with the London VPN server instead of your home internet connection's public IP address. This can change the apparent location associated with your connection, although IP-based location estimates are not always precise.

What Can a VPN Hide?

A VPN changes who can see certain parts of your internet connection, but it does not make all your online activity invisible.

Who is observing? What a VPN generally changes
Your internet service provider A properly configured VPN prevents the ISP from ordinarily reading the contents of traffic protected by the tunnel. The ISP can still see the VPN connection and may observe timing, data volume, and other connection information.
Other people on public Wi-Fi Encryption helps prevent local observers from reading the traffic protected by the VPN tunnel.
Websites you visit Websites typically see the VPN server's public IP address instead of your original public IP address.
Your VPN provider The provider can process traffic passing through its servers and may have access to certain connection information, depending on its design and policies.
Advertisers and tracking services A VPN may reduce tracking based on your IP address, but it does not automatically prevent tracking through cookies, account activity, browser fingerprinting, or other techniques.

The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on VPN applications emphasizes an important point: using a VPN shifts some trust away from your local network and internet provider toward the VPN provider.

For that reason, choosing a trustworthy service matters as much as understanding the technology.

What Is a VPN Used For?

What is a virtual private network used for? Common uses include protecting internet traffic on untrusted networks, accessing private workplace resources, and changing the public IP address that websites see.

Here are some practical examples.

1. Improving Online Privacy

A VPN can make it harder for your internet provider and people monitoring your local network to inspect traffic protected by the VPN tunnel. This is useful when you want another layer of privacy during everyday internet use.

However, a VPN does not prevent every form of tracking. Websites can still recognize you when you sign in to an account or use other identifying information.

2. Adding Protection When Using Public Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi is available in places such as airports, hotels, libraries, and coffee shops.

Although modern websites often use HTTPS encryption, not every application or connection is equally protected. A correctly configured VPN adds encryption between your device and the VPN server, helping protect traffic that might otherwise be exposed to people monitoring the local network.

It is still important to use HTTPS, keep your devices updated, and avoid suspicious links.

3. Connecting Securely to Work Networks

Businesses often use VPNs to let employees connect to internal systems while working from home, traveling, or working at another location. For example, an employee might need to access company files or an internal application that is not available to the general public.

A company VPN can provide an encrypted connection and restrict access to authorized users. Organizations may also use authentication and device security checks to reduce risk. The NIST National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence documents security approaches for mobile devices used to access organizational resources.

4. Accessing Online Services While Traveling

Some websites and online services display different content depending on the apparent location of the connection. A VPN can make your traffic appear to originate from a server in another region. This may help with certain location-dependent services while traveling.

However, a VPN does not guarantee access to particular content. Websites may block VPN connections, require additional verification, or use other methods to determine your location. Access may also be subject to local laws, licensing restrictions, and the service's terms of use.

5. Changing Your Apparent Public IP Address

Websites and online services can use your public IP address to estimate your location or apply certain network restrictions. Because a VPN routes traffic through its server, it can make your connection appear to originate from a different public IP address.

This may be useful when you want to avoid exposing your home connection's IP address directly to every website you visit. Keep in mind that your IP address is only one piece of identifying information. Changing it does not erase other information associated with your online activity.

What Are the Benefits of Using a VPN?

VPNs offer several practical benefits when they are properly configured and operated by a trustworthy provider.

  • An additional layer of connection security. Encryption between your device and the VPN server can help protect traffic from local network monitoring.
  • Greater privacy from your internet provider. Your ISP can generally identify that you are connected to a VPN, but it cannot ordinarily inspect the contents of properly encrypted traffic inside the tunnel.
  • IP address masking. Websites generally see the VPN server's public IP address instead of your original public IP address.
  • Secure remote access. Organizations can use VPNs to connect authorized employees to internal systems without exposing those systems directly to the public internet.
  • More flexibility when traveling. Connecting through a server in another region can change the apparent location of your internet connection, which may be useful for some services.

These advantages can be meaningful, but they should be understood as specific protections rather than a guarantee of complete online security.

What Are the Limitations of a VPN?

A VPN can improve certain aspects of privacy and security, but it cannot solve every online security problem.

A VPN Does Not Make You Completely Anonymous

A VPN can hide your original public IP address from websites, but it does not automatically hide everything that could identify you. Signing in to an account, accepting tracking cookies, sharing personal information, or using an identifiable browser profile can still connect your activity to you.

Websites may also use browser fingerprinting, which involves combining technical details about your browser and device to help recognize them.

A VPN Does Not Prevent Every Cyberattack

A VPN is not a substitute for antivirus protection or safe browsing habits. It does not automatically stop malware infections, phishing attacks, fraudulent websites, or scams that trick you into revealing personal information.

For example, if you receive a fake email asking you to enter your password on a fraudulent website, a VPN will not necessarily recognize the deception or prevent you from entering your credentials.

Basic security practices remain essential. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's Secure Our World guidance covers habits such as recognizing phishing, using strong passwords, enabling multifactor authentication, and keeping software updated. Some CISA pages in this program are archived, so readers should check for current guidance where applicable.

VPN Encryption Has Limits

A VPN's encrypted tunnel typically ends at the VPN server. Traffic traveling beyond that server may still be protected by HTTPS or another encryption protocol, but the VPN tunnel itself does not automatically encrypt every subsequent connection.

This is why HTTPS remains important. As the FTC explains in its online shopping guidance, HTTPS indicates that a website connection is encrypted, but it does not prove that the website itself is legitimate.

Your VPN Provider Becomes an Important Trust Decision

Your VPN provider operates a key part of your connection. Depending on the service's infrastructure, configuration, and privacy practices, it may be able to observe some connection metadata or traffic that is not independently encrypted.

A provider's privacy policy, technical safeguards, data retention practices, and transparency therefore deserve careful attention. A claim that a service keeps no activity logs should be evaluated rather than accepted automatically.

A VPN May Slow Down Your Internet

Using a VPN can reduce connection speeds or increase latency, which is the delay before a response arrives. This may happen because your traffic travels through an additional server, the server is far away, the service is congested, or the encryption process adds overhead.

The effect varies considerably between services, devices, servers, and network conditions. Choosing a nearby server with sufficient capacity may improve performance. For more detail, see Cloudflare's explanation of how VPNs affect internet speed.

Other Security Measures Still Matter

Even when a VPN is enabled, you should continue to use HTTPS websites, install security updates, choose strong and unique passwords, enable multifactor authentication, and be cautious with unexpected messages and downloads.

A VPN works best as one part of a broader approach to online security.

VPN vs. Proxy: What Is the Difference?

VPNs and proxy servers can both route internet traffic through an intermediary, making the destination see the intermediary's IP address rather than the original IP address.

The key differences are how they handle traffic, what they protect, and how they are configured.

Feature VPN Proxy
Main purpose Creates a protected network connection and routes traffic through a VPN server. Forwards selected requests through an intermediary.
Traffic coverage Often covers much of a device's traffic, depending on configuration. Frequently covers a particular application or browser.
Encryption A properly configured VPN encrypts traffic inside its tunnel. Encryption depends on the type of proxy and the protocol used.
IP address masking Usually replaces the visible source IP with the VPN server's IP. Can replace the visible source IP with the proxy's IP.
Common uses Remote work, connection protection, and additional online privacy. Web access, request routing, caching, and other application-specific tasks.

A basic proxy does not necessarily provide the same protection as an encrypted VPN. However, secure proxies can also use encryption, and some VPN-like browser features operate by routing only browser traffic through a proxy.

Coverage is not absolute: some VPNs support split tunneling, which allows selected traffic to bypass the VPN.

For a practical comparison, see Mozilla's explanation of the difference between a VPN and a web proxy.

Which should you choose? A VPN is generally the more appropriate option when you want a protected connection for multiple applications or need secure remote access. A proxy may be sufficient for a specific task that only requires forwarding selected traffic. The right choice depends on the protection you need and the service you are considering.

Free VPN vs. Paid VPN: Which Should You Choose?

Free and paid VPNs can both be useful, but their capabilities, business models, and privacy practices vary.

Free VPNs

A free VPN can be a reasonable starting point when you need basic protection and can find a trustworthy provider.

Potential advantages include:

  • No subscription cost.
  • An opportunity to test a VPN before paying.
  • Sufficient features for occasional or basic use.

Possible disadvantages include data limits, slower speeds, fewer available servers, restricted features, or limits on simultaneous connections.

Some free services may also rely on advertising or collect and share information with third parties. That does not mean every free VPN is unsafe, but it makes evaluating the provider especially important.

Paid VPNs

Paid VPNs often offer more server locations, higher data limits, additional features, and more options for customer support. Depending on the service, these features may be useful for frequent travelers, remote workers, or people who need a more consistent connection.

However, paying for a VPN does not automatically guarantee good privacy or security. A paid provider can still have weak policies, technical shortcomings, or misleading claims.

How to Choose a Trustworthy VPN

Before selecting a service, consider these questions:

  1. What data does it collect? Look for a clear privacy policy that explains what connection information is collected, how long it is retained, and whether it is shared.
  2. Does it properly encrypt traffic? Investigate the protocols and security features the provider supports.
  3. How transparent is the company? Look for meaningful documentation, independent security assessments where available, and clear information about ownership.
  4. Are the limitations acceptable? Review data caps, speed restrictions, server availability, and supported devices.
  5. Does the business model make sense? Understand how the service pays for its infrastructure and what that means for your data.

The FTC's VPN app guidance recommends researching a provider, reviewing application permissions, checking whether traffic is encrypted, and examining how information may be shared.

Ultimately, choose based on the provider's actual practices and the features you need, not simply whether the subscription is free or paid.

Do You Need a VPN?

Whether you need a VPN depends on how you use the internet and which privacy or security risks you want to address.

A VPN may be useful if you frequently connect to public networks, need access to a company's internal network, want to conceal your original public IP address from websites, or prefer to add an encrypted connection between your device and a VPN server.

It may be less important for someone who primarily uses HTTPS websites on trusted networks and does not need the additional features a VPN provides.

That does not mean VPNs are unnecessary. It means their usefulness depends on your circumstances.

Before subscribing, consider whether a VPN addresses a specific need you have. Then evaluate the provider's privacy practices and security features.

You may find that a reputable free service meets your requirements. Alternatively, a paid plan may be worthwhile if it offers features that matter to you.

There is no universal requirement for every internet user to purchase a VPN.

Frequently Asked Questions About VPNs

1. What Is a VPN in Simple Terms?

A VPN is a technology that creates an encrypted connection between your device and a VPN server. It helps protect the traffic sent through that connection and makes websites see the VPN server's public IP address instead of your original public IP address.

2. Is Using a VPN Legal?

VPNs are legal in many countries, but laws and restrictions vary by jurisdiction. Some governments restrict certain VPN services or uses. Check the current rules where you live or travel, and remember that using a VPN does not make otherwise unlawful activity legal. The Internet Society's overview of VPN restrictions discusses differences between jurisdictions.

3. Does a VPN Hide My IP Address?

A VPN typically hides your original public IP address from the websites you visit by routing your traffic through a VPN server. However, it does not necessarily hide your identity, account activity, cookies, or other identifying information.

4. Can a VPN Protect Me on Public Wi-Fi?

A properly configured VPN helps protect traffic inside its encrypted tunnel from people monitoring a public Wi-Fi network. It does not eliminate every threat, so you should still use HTTPS, update your devices, and avoid suspicious links and downloads.

5. Can a VPN Make Me Completely Anonymous?

No. A VPN can improve privacy, but websites may still recognize you through accounts, cookies, browser fingerprinting, and information you voluntarily provide. Your VPN provider also becomes an important part of your privacy model.

6. Does a VPN Slow Down My Internet?

It can. Routing traffic through an additional server, server congestion, distance, and encryption overhead may reduce speeds or increase latency. The actual effect depends on your connection and the VPN service you use.

7. Is a Free VPN Safe?

Some free VPNs can be suitable, but others may have weak security, intrusive data collection, or unclear privacy policies. Evaluate each provider individually and avoid assuming that a service is trustworthy simply because it is free—or that it is secure because it is paid.

8. Should I Leave My VPN On All the Time?

You can leave a reputable VPN enabled continuously if you value the additional privacy and it does not interfere with your activities. Keeping it on may help ensure that supported traffic remains protected. However, some services may require a local connection, may block VPN traffic, or may work differently depending on your settings. Decide when to use it based on your needs and the service's requirements.

Conclusion

A VPN is a useful technology that creates an encrypted connection between your device and a VPN server. It can help protect traffic on public networks, improve privacy from your internet provider, support secure remote work, and change the public IP address that websites see.

However, a VPN is not a guarantee of anonymity or complete protection from cyber threats. Your provider's privacy practices matter, your connection may become slower, and tracking methods such as cookies and account-based identification can still reveal information about you.

The best approach is to understand what a VPN protects, recognize its limitations, and combine it with other security practices such as using HTTPS, maintaining updated devices, choosing strong passwords, and enabling multifactor authentication.

The main takeaway

A VPN can be a valuable part of your online security toolkit, but the right choice depends on your privacy goals, your browsing habits, and the trustworthiness of the service you select.
